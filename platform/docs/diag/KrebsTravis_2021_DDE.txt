                                               Abstract

A novel potential flow-based method for unsteady aerodynamic analysis with relaxed wakes is presented.

This method models aerodynamic surfaces and wakes using distributed doublet elements (DDEs) of

parabolic strength in both the spanwise and streamwise directions. Due to the nature of the method, the

velocities induced by a DDE-based system are bounded everywhere, meaning there are no non-physical

or infinite induced velocities in the velocity field, as opposed to lower-order filament-based potential flow

methods. In addition to providing a robust velocity field, the use of DDEs provides a degree of stability

in predicting unsteady forces that is unachievable with conventional lower-order methods.

   The DDE-based method was validated through a series of four studies. The first study predicted

the span efficiency of various planar elliptical wings, which agreed with the classical lifting line solution

to within 3%. The second study predicted the unsteady lift response to an airfoil passing through a

sharp-edged gust, which matched the analytical solution provided by the Küssner function, illustrating

the stability of the unsteady force calculations within the DDE-based method. The third study predicted

the unsteady time-averaged thrust force produced by a small, rigid rotor at various tip-path plane angles

and advance ratios, matching experimentally-obtained data to within 10%. The final study compared

the transient sectional thrust of a small, rigid rotor blade operating under unsteady conditions to those

obtained via a CFD-based analysis. The comparison agreed well in magnitude and azimuthal location.

   Two exploratory studies focusing on small, rigid rotors were performed to demonstrate potential

applications of the DDE-based method. The first study quantified the impacts of rolling maneuvers

                                                     iii
on the time-averaged thrust and moments of a rigid rotor, with the results indicating that a rolling

maneuver can have a significant effect on the thrust and roll moment coefficients. The second study

predicted the transient thrust of two rotors operating under multiple tip-path plane angles and advance

ratios, showing transient thrust oscillations of up to 60% of the mean thrust output in some cases. The

thrust output of individual rotor blades oscillated by up to 90% of the mean total rotor thrust under

certain conditions.

Chapter 1


Introduction

Computational methods for aerodynamic analysis are vital for investigating and quantifying the perfor-

mance of aerodynamic bodies. They provide a valuable supplement to, or substitute for, experimental

testing which can be prohibitively expensive. There are various computational methods available, each

with their own strengths and weaknesses due to their governing methods and the assumptions applied to

the analysis. As a general rule, the higher the degree of exactness with which a flow is represented, the

higher the computational expense. Due to this, balancing the capability and expense of a computational

method is an important consideration during the selection process.

   Most computational methods for aerodynamic analysis approximate solutions to the Navier-Stokes

equations in order to predict flow field properties. The implementation and the fidelity of this approxi-

mation is one way with which methods can be categorized. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD)-based

methods typically discretize the flow field into cells of finite volume over which the Navier-Stokes equa-

tions can be numerically solved. The approximations and assumptions made to the system can vary

widely, with one example being the inclusion or exclusion of viscous effects. The breadth to the applica-

bility and capability of computational fluid dynamics makes it a valuable tool with which aerodynamic

systems can be analyzed. However, these methods typically require extensive computational power and

time to prepare and solve, often limiting their applications to more in-depth analyses.

   An example of the complexity of implementing a high-fidelity CFD-based analysis is in the control

volume discretization, or grid generation process. This is a resource-intensive process that requires

specialized skills and is critically important to the solution stability and accuracy (1). The grid charac-

                                                    1
teristics and sizing needed for an accurate analysis are dependent on the geometry, analysis parameters,

and the assumptions made regarding the governing equations. These factors drive requirements such as

the grid resolution necessary near the surface, which determines the effectiveness of the boundary layer

modelling. As a result of this grid dependence, a solution’s sensitivity to the grid sizing must typically

be quantified through grid independence studies (2).

   In addition to the complexity of the grid generation, the solution time for a three-dimensional CFD-

based analysis is often measured in days or weeks, and may require the use of supercomputing clusters.

In order to reduce the computational expense of a CFD-based approach, simplifications can be made

to the governing Navier-Stokes equations. The assumption of adiabatic and inviscid flow, for example,

reduces the Navier-Stokes equations to the Euler equations (3), reducing the required grid complexity

and computational expense of the solution. Euler methods are typically applied to analyses where

boundary layer effects are considered insignificant, such as supersonic flows (1).

   Potential flow-based methods are derived from even further simplifications to the Navier-Stokes

equations, making assumptions that the flow field is irrotational, inviscid, and typically incompressible.

Aerodynamic systems are represented using singularity elements placed along the surfaces, with the

superpositioning of their effects forming the flow field. Due to the simplified governing equations and

the surface-based singularity representation rather than the volume-based approach of higher-fidelity

methods, potential flow-based analyses require substantially less time and computational power to run

when compared to CFD-based methods (4). A trade-off of this decreased computational expense is that

potential flow-based methods are often unsuitable for analyses with significant viscous contributions

(1). Though the simplifications made to their governing equations limit the applicability of potential

flow-based methods, they remain the basis of many widely-used computationally-inexpensive prediction

methods for aircraft and aerodynamic surfaces.



1.1     Motivation

Potential flow-based methods provide aircraft designers with a relatively fast way of quantifying and

qualifying a design’s performance early on in the design process (4). The runtime of these methods

is typically counted in minutes, and the low resource requirements lend it to parallelization, meaning

multiple analyses can be run simultaneously. In addition to this, the integrated nature of the aircraft


                                                    2
design process has driven interest in optimization-based approaches. Capable and fast aerodynamic

performance prediction methods can be teamed with structural models and multi-objective optimization

algorithms to create efficient designs based on specific criteria, with examples given by Refs. (5; 6; 7;

8). However, the applicability of these optimization-based approaches are constrained by the suitability

of the aerodynamic performance prediction method used. Optimizations typically require hundreds

of thousands of design evaluations, meaning that using high-fidelity CFD-based methods within an

optimization-based approach is often not feasible due to the time and computational power required.

For these reasons, potential flow-based methods still play an important role in aircraft conceptual design

and analysis.

   Recent decades have seen substantial growth in the adoption of unconventional aircraft configurations

such as multirotor and vertical takeoff and landing (VTOL) aircraft, due mainly to the emergence

of both the drone and urban air mobility industries. The electrification of aircraft propulsion has

also spurred interest in distributed propulsion for fixed-wing aircraft, as is the case with the X-57

Maxwell pictured in Fig. 1.1 and discussed in detail by Borer et al. (9). Due largely to the X-57

Maxwell project, the AIAA introduced the Workshop for Integrated Propeller Prediction (WIPP) in

2019, which is dedicated to analyzing integrated propeller-wing systems using CFD-based methods (10).

The unconventional nature of these modern aircraft configurations poses interesting design and analysis

challenges for aerodynamicists, as they incorporate a multitude of rotors and/or propellers in close

proximity to one another, and to other aerodynamic surfaces such as wings.




                                   Figure 1.1: The X-57 Maxwell (11)


   The complexity of analyzing these aircraft configurations exists even at the component level. Many

remotely-piloted aircraft systems and urban air mobility vehicles use rigid rotors, which must operate

under a wide-range of inflow conditions. During the ascending and descending stages of flight, these

rotors have largely axial flow, meaning that the majority of the inflow into the rotors is in-line with their

                                                     3
axes of rotation. Under these conditions, the rotors are acting in a predominantly steady-state manner,

similar to a conventional forward-facing propeller. While the vehicle is in forward flight, however, the

rotor planes are typically acute to the incoming flow. Due to this, advancing and retreating blade

effects act on the individual rotor blades, and the overall forces and moments generated by the rotors

are transient. The transient nature of these rotor loads necessitates an unsteady analysis framework,

whether analyzing individual isolated rotors or the aircraft configuration as a whole.

   Existing unsteady potential flow-based methods are limited in their applicability when analyzing

these unconventional systems. Strong interactions between aerodynamic surfaces and shed wakes, as is

the case with rotors in forward flight or integrated propeller-wing systems, are a source of instability

for these methods. These instabilities are discussed in further detail in Chapter 2. As a result of these

limitations, systems of this nature are typically analyzed using CFD-based methods. In a notable recent

study, Misiorowski et al. (12) quantified the transient thrust oscillations of a single isolated rigid rotor

at one operating condition, using an unsteady Navier-Stokes solver. This was done in the course of

quantifying rotor interactions on quadrotors. In a similar vein, McQuaid et al. (13) analyzed both an

isolated rotor and a quadcopter as a means of investigating the onset of vortex ring state. Both of

these studies required the use of supercomputing clusters. Cerny and Breitsamter (14) used an unsteady

Navier-Stokes solver to analyze a propeller operating at seven different inflow angles, comparing the

results to experimentally-obtained data. Outside of these three studies, the unsteady aerodynamic

analysis of small rotors is notably lacking from the literature.

   Regarding the analysis of integrated propeller-wing systems, the AIAA Workshop for Integrated

Propeller Prediction has spurred interest in the research of propeller-wing interactions using CFD-based

methods, with examples given by Refs. (15; 16; 17; 18). This research area is still emerging, with the

WIPP representatives noting that the “effort represents a critical first step in the ability to accurately

predict propeller effects with confidence as it culminates in an open powered test database that can be

used for CFD validation” (19). While the application of CFD-based methods to these unconventional and

unsteady systems is maturing, potential flow-based methods could potentially provide insights into the

effects of airfoil camber, thickness, or wing planform shape (4), which can help to identify broader trends

in the design space. Without computationally-inexpensive and stable unsteady performance prediction

methods, the design and analysis of unconventional aircraft configurations is hampered.

The purpose of this research is to introduce a new potential flow-based method for the transient analysis

of complex and unsteady aerodynamic systems with relaxed wakes. As such, this method aims to

support the analysis and design of unconventional and unsteady systems, including rotors with advancing

Potential Flow

Potential flow theory forms the basis of many computationally-inexpensive aerodynamic performance

prediction methods. Potential flow theory predated digital computers and was founded on the assumption

of an irrotational velocity field as a necessary simplification to the Navier-Stokes equations at the time

(20). Coupled with the assumption that the flow is inviscid and incompressible, potential flow methods

were initially intended to be computationally-inexpensive enough for human computers to use. As digital

computers have grown more powerful, the scope and capabilities of potential flow-based methods have in-

creased while their runtimes have decreased (21). The capabilities and the computationally-inexpensive

nature of their design have kept them an appropriate choice for many aerodynamic performance predic-

tion applications. The increasing use of optimization-based design approaches has shown that potential

flow-based methods are still indispensable.



2.1     Characteristics of Potential Flow-Based Methods

There can be significant differences in the capability and computational expense of two different ap-

proaches among potential flow-based methods, depending on how the systems are modelled and which

assumptions are made. Simpler and faster methods typically use lumped vorticity in the form of vor-

tex filaments (22), while more comprehensive methods distribute vorticity over a surface (23). Some

approaches model both the upper and lower surfaces of the wing or rotor to account for thickness (24),

while others only model the camber line or zero-lift line (25). Wake representations may also vary be-


                                                    6
tween methods, with some using fixed or prescribed wake geometries and others using a relaxed wake

model, which evolves the wake shape over time. Lastly, how aerodynamic loads are calculated is an

important distinction, with some methods relying on pressure integration and others relying directly on

the singularity strength.


2.1.1    Singularity Elements

Potential flow-based methods use singularity elements to model the flow field. These elements are placed

along the aerodynamic surfaces and other boundaries, such as the wake shear layer. The superpositioning

of their effects forms a vector field approximation of the flow, which can then be used to approximate

aerodynamic loads. Depending on the element type, the strength of a singularity element can be lumped

into a point or line, or distributed over a surface. The various types of singularity elements have their

own strengths and weaknesses, which can define the applicability of the overall method. The following

section will give an overview of several commonly-used singularity elements along with their advantages

and disadvantages, which provides important context for this research.

Vortex Sheets and Distributed Normal-Doublets

The lumped vorticity contained within a vortex filament, represented by the circulation strength Γ,

can be distributed linearly across an infinitely-thin planar surface as vorticity, γ(ξ, η), forming what is

known as a vortex sheet.1 The distributed nature of a vortex sheet results in a velocity field that is

bounded everywhere, meaning that vortex sheets do not induce infinite velocities as long as the sheet is

continuous (28). However, the induced velocities can become singular at the perimeter of a sheet with

non-zero strength, in the same way that the velocity induced by a vortex filament at its core is singular

(28). Additionally, the tangentially-induced velocity of a vortex sheet is discontinuous across the plane

of the sheet (27)(28).

    The use of vortex sheets rather than vortex filaments removes the potential for infinite induced

velocities, which is desirable for applications where a solid-core correction factor for the vortex filaments

is inadequate. However, implementing a completely vortex sheet-based system in three-dimensions is

complicated by the fact that the vorticity distribution must be divergence-free, as it is defined as the

divergence of the curl of the velocity field, which is by definition zero for potential flow fields (27).

Prescribing a divergence-free vorticity distribution directly to a vortex sheet is nontrivial, which is why

purely vortex sheet-based solutions are typically not used in three-dimensional analyses (28).

    Drela (28) outlines one method by which to achieve a divergence-free vorticity distribution, through

first prescribing a higher-order scalar function µ(ξ, η) which acts as a stream function for the vorticity.

To achieve a linearly-varying vorticity distribution, then, the scalar function µ(ξ, η) must be second-

order. The surface gradient of this scalar function µ(ξ, η), rotated 90◦ about the surface unit normal,

defines the vorticity:

of a distributed normal-doublet sheet, referred to hereafter as simply a distributed doublet sheet. That

is to say, a vortex sheet with a divergence-free vorticity distribution γ(ξ, η) is functionally equivalent to

a distributed doublet sheet of strength µ(ξ, η). Due to this equivalence, and due to the complications

Equivalence of Circulation and Doublet Strength Distributions

An important takeaway from the relationship between vortex sheets and distributed doublet sheets is

the conditional equivalence of the doublet strength distribution, µ(ξ, η), to the circulation distribution,

Γ(ξ, η). A distributed doublet sheet can be viewed as an infinite series of infinitesimally small constant-

strength doublet sheets of varying strength represented by the distribution µ(ξ, η). As stated previously,

constant-strength doublet sheets and constant-strength vortex rings are functionally identical, meaning

that the terms µ(ξ, η) and Γ(ξ, η) may be used interchangeably under certain conditions.

   If the higher-order doublet strength distribution µ(ξ, η) is forced to be continuous and never permitted

to have a step-change to zero, the integration of the resulting vorticity distribution defined by Eq. 2.2

would yield an expression representing both µ(ξ, η) and Γ(ξ, η). The relationship between the doublet

strength and vorticity distributions under these conditions is equivalent to the relationship between the

circulation and vorticity distributions. That is to say, it is conditionally acceptable for the definition of

the vorticity introduced in Eq. 2.2 to be based on either the doublet strength or circulation distribution:

2.1.2     Representations of Aerodynamic Surfaces

There are two ways in which singularity elements are commonly arranged to represent aerodynamic

surfaces. The first is known as a panel method, which models the aerodynamic surfaces with thickness.

The singularity elements are placed along both the upper and lower portions of the aerodynamic surfaces,

forming a closed-body (27). Panel methods aim to discretize the geometry with high fidelity. The second

commonly-used method is known as a lifting surface method, which models the aerodynamic surfaces

as infinitely thin. Singularity elements are typically placed along the zero-lift line or camber line of the


                                                      11
airfoil (1)(27), as shown in Fig. 2.4 which illustrates a wing and wake modelled with vortex rings using

a lifting surface method.




        Figure 2.4: A representation of a wing and wake with vortex rings. Recreated from (27).


   Lifting surface methods are typically less computationally-expensive than panel methods, as they

require fewer surface elements (1). In addition to this, using a zero-thickness model avoids any issues

that could arise from wake piercing, where wake elements enter into the interior of a closed body, as

can be observed with panel codes and strong aerodynamic interactions (30). Using a relaxed wake

model within a panel method can be problematic, as the aerodynamic surfaces’ internal regions have a

constant velocity potential and, therefore, stagnant flow. As a result, any relaxed wake elements that

pierce the body will indefinitely remain within the interior region, leading to a non-physical flow field.

As the purpose of this research is to develop a method for the analysis of complex systems with strong

surface-wake interactions, this research will focus primarily on lifting surface based representations.


2.1.3     Representations of Wakes

The manner in which the strength and shape of the wake are prescribed and how these characteristics

change with time are important areas of distinction. As a solution progresses, wake elements are shed

from the trailing edges of the surfaces. The strengths assigned to newly-created wake elements are equal

to the strength of the trailing edge surface elements from which they are shed (27). For a conventional

steady-state analysis in which the flow conditions are unchanging, the strength of the surface elements


                                                    12
do not change with time once the singularity element strengths have converged. As a result of this, the

strengths of the corresponding wake elements are unchanging in the streamwise direction. As a simplifi-

cation for steady-state analysis, the trailing edge surface element strength will typically be propagated

down the entire wake every timestep, resulting in a wake of constant strength in the streamwise direction

(27)(31). This is illustrated in Fig. 2.4, where the circulation strength of the wake elements, ΓW , are set

to be equal in the streamwise direction. This type of steady-state analysis and simplified wake-strength

representation are typically applied to time-independent systems such as conventional wings, or rotors

in purely axial flow.

   For time-dependent solutions, the strengths of the surface elements change as the flow conditions

change. To properly capture unsteady wake effects, the strengths of the individual wake elements

must remain constant once they are shed and the solution progresses. This results in a wake that has

streamwise changes in strength, representative of the changes in strength of the surface elements as

they contend with changing flow conditions. The upper portion of Fig. 2.5 illustrates an unsteady

two-dimensional airfoil and wake, with the streamwise wake vorticity given as γw (t). Represented by

discrete vortices in this example, a discretized wake is illustrated in the lower portion of Fig. 2.5, with

vortices of varying strength ΓW spaced at intervals equivalent to the distance travelled by the airfoil over

a timestep. The strengths assigned to these vortices are based on the integrated wake vorticity across

that timestep, resulting from the flow conditions acting on the airfoil during that timestep.




Figure 2.5: An example of the discretization of an unsteady two-dimensional wake. Recreated from (27).


   Modelling streamwise changes in wake strength is a necessary component of an unsteady analysis,

as the strength of the wake is a crucial factor in quantifying the influence that it holds over the surface

elements. The wake strength also impacts its shape when using a relaxed wake model, which moves


                                                    13
the wake elements with the velocity field. For steady-state analyses, the relaxation procedure often

involves modifying an existing prescribed wake geometry iteratively until the wake shape converges (27).

Alternatively, this relaxation procedure can be incorporated into the timestepping loop so that the wake

elements are moved with their local velocities as the solution progresses, which is a necessary approach

for time-dependent unsteady analyses (27).


2.1.4    Force Calculations

There are several ways in which the steady and unsteady aerodynamic forces can be approximated

within a potential flow-based system. A common method for all analysis types is pressure integration,

which relates the surface velocities acting on the system to the surface pressures and, subsequently, the

forces. Also applicable to both steady and unsteady analyses is the Kutta-Joukowski theorem, which

relates the singularity strength of the elements and the magnitude and direction of the flow field to the

resulting force vector. Unsteady effects can also be approximated using indicial response methods, which

take the unsteady response of a two-dimensional airfoil undergoing a change in aerodynamic boundary

conditions, and apply it to a three-dimensional section of an aerodynamic surface. A brief description

of these three methods follows.

Numerical Instabilities in Unsteady Force Calculations

The prediction of unsteady forces can be challenging when using conventional vortex filament-based

methods. One such example is the ratio of timestep size to distance travelled by the surface elements.

Simpson and Palacios (39) used an unsteady vortex lattice method (UVLM) outlined by Katz and

Plotkin (27), and found that newly shed wake panels should be of approximately equal area compared

to the trailing-edge surface elements from which they inherit their circulation strength. That is to say,

the distance travelled by the surface elements each timestep, denoted by ∆xw , should be approximately

equal to the chord length of the trailing edge surface element, denoted by ∆xc . The reference to this

length-ratio constraint appears to be related to the work of Rusak et al. (40), who note that vortex-lattice

methods tend to become unstable when the wake-element length is less than that of the surface element.

A likely reason for this instability is the finite-differencing methods typically used within UVLMs to

determine the apparent mass term, introduced in Eq. 2.8 (27). However, enforcing a timestep size

constraint such as ∆xw /∆xc ≈ 1 while analyzing rotors is problematic since the distance travelled by

the surface elements of rotors depends on their distance from the axis of rotation. Forward flight, with

edgewise flight components, compounds this problem as the distance travelled by surface elements in

these cases also depends on the rotor blades’ azimuthal location.


Indicial Response Methods

Some unsteady potential flow methods, such as RCAS (41), use an indicial response method to approx-

imate unsteady forces. These methods model the response of a two-dimensional airfoil to a step-change

in aerodynamic boundary conditions, such as a change in angle of attack or a plunging motion, and

incorporate the effects of shed circulation and apparent mass (42). This response is superimposed over

time to model the effects of a series of sequential changes to the boundary conditions. The resulting two-

dimensional unsteady response is then used to approximate the unsteady response of a three-dimensional

wing section within a strip method (43). Because indicial response methods incorporate the effects of

shed circulation, the wake strength at one spanwise location does not influence the unsteady forces acting

on the surface at another spanwise location (43). Both pressure integration and the unsteady Kutta-

Joukowski formulation do not have this limitation, as they model the effects of the shed circulation in

three-dimensions. Further information on indicial response methods are given by (43; 44; 42).

2.2     Existing Potential Flow Methods for Unsteady Analysis

The following sections give an overview of the most relevant potential flow-based methods for unsteady

aerodynamic analysis, which form the basis for many specialized codes used to analyze unsteady wings

and rotors. Understanding the advantages and disadvantages of these methods provides important

context for the motivation behind this research.


2.2.1    The Unsteady Vortex Lattice Method

One of the more common potential flow methods for unsteady analysis is the unsteady vortex lattice

method (UVLM), outlined by Katz and Plotkin (27). This method uses constant-strength vortex rings

to model lifting surfaces and wakes, similar to the system illustrated in Fig. 2.4. Individual wake

element strengths are held constant over time, modelling streamwise changes in wake strength through

a method similar to that shown in Fig. 2.5. Due to the UVLM modelling the system using vortex rings,

it is typically the faster option when compared to other unsteady aerodynamic performance prediction

methods, including other potential flow-based methods.

   One drawback to UVLM-based methods is their reliance on vortex filaments, which necessitate cor-

rection factors to reduce the potential for infinite induced velocities. As these correction factors are

only utilized when determining the velocities near vortex filaments, they typically do not impact the

solution (27). Velocities in potential flow-based methods are commonly calculated at element control

points, usually placed at the center of the vortex rings and, therefore, an adequate distance from the

vortex filaments at the perimeter of the element. However, issues can arise when the edge of one element

is in close proximity to another element’s control point, which can happen with strong wake-on-wake

or surface-wake interactions, as is the case with complex systems such as rotors in forward flight or

propeller-wing systems.

   Further information is provided by Refs. (45; 46; 47), which include examples of unsteady applications

of the UVLM method. The unsteady vortex lattice method has also been extended and improved upon

in various ways, including using vortex particle wake models (48; 49; 50), and modelling leading-edge

vortex shedding (51).

2.2.2    The Distributed Vorticity Element Method

Based on the multiple-lifting line method developed by Horstmann (52), the distributed vorticity element

(DVE) method introduced by Bramesfeld (36) and Bramesfeld and Maughmer (25) is a variation on the

conventional vortex-lattice method. In essence, the DVE method uses vortex sheets as a replacement for

the discrete trailing vortices present in traditional vortex lattice methods. A typical element from the

DVE method is shown in Fig. 2.6. These elements consist of a leading and trailing edge vortex filament

of equal and opposite quadratic circulation strength along the element span, with a vortex sheet in

between that varies linearly in the spanwise direction, modelling the shed vorticity from the quadratic

filaments. Conceptually, a single DVE can be thought of as a vortex ring-based system with infinite

spanwise elements. Using these vortex sheets reduces the number of filaments in the system and reduces

the potential for infinite or singular induced velocities. In addition to this, the second-order spanwise

circulation distribution of a DVE requires fewer elements to model spanwise changes in circulation when

compared to constant-strength vortex rings.

is represented entirely with vortex sheets, which have constant strength in the streamwise direction

and change in a first-order continuous manner in the spanwise direction. With the wake free of vortex

of a steady-state relaxed wake analysis is illustrated in Fig. 2.7, which models a wing half-span using

   As surface elements still have bound vortices at their leading and trailing edges, steady-state analyses

with DVE-based methods are subject to certain instabilities. For example, a relaxed wake analysis can

become unstable if a wake element passes near a surface element, as it can encounter a large induced

velocity and deform unpredictably. Independent of the wake model, if two surface elements are arranged

closely together, the induced velocities at these control points can be influenced heavily by the vortex

filaments. Due to these bound vortices, steady-state analyses with DVE-based methods are not entirely

free of the instabilities present in VLM-based methods.

   Further issues arise with DVE-based methods during unsteady analysis, where the bound vortices are

needed in the wake to model streamwise changes in circulation. Unsteady analysis with the DVE method

requires a fixed wake model, as relaxed wake interactions are erratic when the bound vortices are included

in the wake. Even with a fixed wake model, unsteady systems with strong surface-wake interactions can

result in issues as the vortex filaments in the wake may pass near the surface elements’ control points

and cause large induced velocities that impact the solution. As a result, unsteady aerodynamic analyses

with DVE-based methods face similar issues to UVLM-based methods.

   The DVE method has been applied to both fixed-wing and rotorcraft analysis. Barcelos et al. (54)

used the DVE method with a relaxed wake model to analyze quadrotor flight configurations as a way


                                                    20
of quantifying the impact of rotor-on-rotor interactions. This analysis was done under a steady-state

framework, as the relaxed wake model is unstable when modelling streamwise changes in circulation, as

previously discussed. Cole (32) used the DVE method to analyze propeller-wing interactions, using a

steady-state relaxed wake for the propeller and a fully-unsteady fixed wake for the wing. This choice of

wake models was a necessary compromise, as a fully-unsteady propeller wake would introduce potential

instabilities when interacting with the wing surface, while a steady-state relaxed wake model for the

wing would neglect the unsteady nature of the propeller-wing interaction. More detail on using the

distributed vorticity element method in unsteady analysis is provided by Cole et al. (43) (55).


2.2.3      Higher-Order Distributed-Strength Methods

A well-known higher-order distributed element-based potential flow method is PANAIR. It uses first-

order source and second-order doublet distributions across quadrilateral surface elements (23), meaning

that the system is represented entirely with higher-order elements and does not require the use of

vortex filaments. The higher-order distributed nature of the elements used in PANAIR are inherently

more computationally expensive than the vortex ring counterparts from methods such as the UVLM.

PANAIR can be used as both a panel method or lifting surface method, and the forces and moments

in PANAIR-based methods are typically determined using pressure integration. However, PANAIR

assumes a quasi-steady framework (56), limiting its applicability for the analysis of complex unsteady

systems.

   The induction model introduced by Johnson (23) was used by Pate (57), and Pate and German (24),

who applied it to a panel method for unsteady analysis. The method models the surface and wake solely

with linearly-varying vortex sheets, meaning that the strength distribution relies on directly determining

a divergence-free vorticity distribution as discussed in Section 2.1.1. The purpose of this method is to

model accurate surface velocities for an unsteady analysis (57), and as such it is strictly a panel method.



2.3        Purpose of this Research

The purpose of this research is to develop a robust potential flow-based method for the analysis of

unsteady systems with relaxed wakes. This is accomplished through the development of an entirely new

distributed doublet-based method that removes all reliance on vortex filaments, which form the basis of


                                                    21
many instabilities in the UVLM and DVE-based approaches. With the surface and wake represented

using distributed doublet sheets of parabolic strength distribution in both the spanwise and streamwise

directions, systems modelled with the distributed doublet element (DDE)-based method contain no

lumped vorticity, even when modelling unsteady wakes. In addition to this, the DDE-based method

includes the modelling of both the shed circulation and the non-circulatory apparent mass, constituting

a fully-unsteady framework. As a result of this framework and the entirely distributed nature of the

system, stable unsteady relaxed wake analyses are possible even when the systems contain strong surface-

wake or surface-on-surface interactions.


2.3.1    Research Contributions

The DDE-based method introduced in the herein study re-purposes the second-order distributed doublet

induction model outlined by Johnson (23) into a fully-unsteady lifting surface method, as a natural

progression to the UVLM, DVE, and PANAIR methods. Combining the stability and distributed nature

of these second-order planar doublet elements into a lifting surface method framework allows it to be

used for fully-unsteady relaxed wake analysis, while still retaining some of the benefits of the lifting

surface method in terms of computational expense and complexity.

The Distributed Doublet Element

Method

The Distributed Doublet Element (DDE) method was developed to analyze complex and unsteady

systems with relaxed wakes, which is challenging or unfeasible with existing filament-based methods.

The DDE-based approach improves upon lower-fidelity methods by distributing the vorticity over planar

surfaces in both the streamwise and spanwise directions, rather than lumping the strengths into vortex

filaments. As a result of this distribution and unlike lower-fidelity methods, the velocity field induced

by a DDE-based system is bounded everywhere. This gives the DDE method a distinct advantage in

modelling these unsteady systems.

   An overview of the DDE-based algorithm is shown in Fig. 3.1. To begin, the wing or rotor is

discretized into thin surfaces using planar triangular elements that are placed along the camber line.

The initial doublet strength distributions for these elements are determined by imposing flow tangency

conditions along the surface, and necessitating the strength distribution be continuous across all elements

on a surface. A timestepping procedure then commences, and a row of triangular wake elements are

created at the trailing edge of the surface. The strength distribution for the surface and newly-created

wake elements are determined, and the wake is relaxed. The strength distribution for the entire surface

and wake is updated once again to account for the movement and stretching of the wake elements. The




                                                    23
forces acting on the surface elements are then calculated. The next timestep begins, and this process

continues until a user-specified timestep limit is reached.

3.2     Representation of Lifting Surfaces and Wakes

All surface and wakes in the DDE method are represented using distributed doublet elements, meaning

all elements in the system follow the relationship outlined in Eq. 3.3. All surfaces and wakes are modelled

with zero-thickness, with surface elements placed along the wing, rotor, or propeller camber line. An

example of a system modelled with DDEs is displayed in Fig. 3.3, which shows a wing half-span with a

4-bladed wingtip-mounted propeller. As the DDEs are triangular, non-planar surfaces such as camber

lines of wings or rotors are modelled as gapless surfaces. Although the DDE method can technically

model thickness effects similar to a panel method, it is intended to model complex systems with strong

surface-wake interactions. Thus, for the purposes of this research, the lifting surfaces are modelled with

zero thickness to avoid any wake piercing.

3.3     Determination of the Circulation Strength Distribution

As previously stated, due to the nature of distributed doublet elements, any stepwise change in the

circulation along the edge of an element or between two neighbouring elements is equivalent to a vortex

filament with a strength equal to that stepwise change (28). However, if the triangular elements are joined

together with a continuous circulation distribution across neighbouring elements, there are no stepwise

changes, and therefore the velocity induced by that system will be bounded everywhere. Due to this

fact, the circulation distribution across the system must be continuous. This distribution is determined

via the boundary conditions imposed on the system, which are, as a result, focused on removing any


                                                    27
stepwise change in strength. At the leading edge and free ends of lifting surfaces, the circulation strength

is prescribed to zero. Between neighbouring elements, the circulation and vorticity are prescribed to

be continuous to facilitate a smooth transition in strength across the junction. At the trailing edge of

lifting surfaces, the circulation strength is carried into the wake. As an example, a circulation distribution

fulfilling these boundary conditions is illustrated in Fig. 3.5, in which a graphical representation of the

circulation distribution is superimposed over the corresponding planar elliptical wing.

centroid of the element and the 90% location from the centroid to each of the three vertices, as shown in

Fig. 3.6. These flow tangency conditions imposed near the element vertices ensure that at junctions of

multiple elements, such as a single vertex connected to multiple DDEs, flow tangency is enforced in the

immediate area. An example of this is illustrated in Fig. 3.7, which shows a top-down view of a simple

rectangular wing consisting of eight elements. Eight collocation points surround the central vertex of

the wing. While the tangential velocities at these points may differ, the normal velocity components are

distribution is closely tied to the self-induced normal velocity of an element.

elements should be required to model a surface when compared to conventional vortex-lattice methods.

However, the accuracy of a solution is tied to the number of flow tangency conditions on the surface.

Thus, by increasing the number of collocation points per element, the total number of elements required

tangency conditions provides comprehensive coverage of the wing while taking advantage of the reduced

number of elements needed due to the higher-order circulation distribution.

   As there are six circulation-strength coefficients per DDE, the number of boundary conditions for a

perfectly-constrained system is 6NE , where NE represents the number of surface elements in the system.

This ideally-constrained system would produce a circulation distribution that satisfies all boundary con-

ditions completely. In actuality, the DDE-method is over-constrained. The excess number of boundary

conditions is a result of prescribing a continuous higher-order circulation distribution across an entire

surface. Prescribing a gapless second-order circulation distribution between two DDEs requires three

boundary conditions. In addition to this, the gradient of the circulation distribution is set equal at the

edge mid-point between neighbouring elements in both the ξ and η-directions, resulting in a further

two boundary conditions. As such, a typical single-edge boundary between two DDEs can require a

total of five boundary conditions, or 2.5 boundary conditions per edge per DDE. With the system being

over-constrained, the element strengths are solved by finding the minimum norm least-squares solution.

Due to this approximation, the element strengths are not guaranteed to satisfy every boundary con-

dition completely, and the network may feature discontinuities in the circulation distribution. These

discontinuities must be corrected; otherwise, singularities remain in the system.

   As noted by Johnson (23), it is virtually impossible to define a second-order doublet distribution

(analogous to the circulation distribution for this application) that is continuous across all panel edges

of a network. To remove discontinuities, any gaps in the circulation distribution between elements are

removed through averaging, and the leading edge and free-end strengths are prescribed to zero. This

results in a continuous strength distribution. These corrections, however, impact the system’s adherence

to the flow tangency conditions. To mitigate this effect, the error introduced by this correction is quan-

tified through re-evaluating the flow tangency conditions based on the updated strength distribution.

This process iterates as the prediction-correction algorithm outlined in Fig. 3.8. The prediction is the

calculation of the least-squares solution, and the correction is the removal of discontinuities. This algo-

rithm continues iterating until the solution converges to a continuous circulation distribution, determined

through monitoring the maximum change in circulation occurring across the surface elements every it-

eration. With the convergence of the continuous circulation distribution, any error within the system is

then reflected in deviation from the flow tangency conditions, which is quantified with a coefficient of

determination at the conclusion of the procedure outlined in Fig. 3.8.

trailing edge of the aerodynamic surface. The freestream and induced forces are found by integrating the

Kutta-Joukowski theorem across elements, while the non-circulatory forces are approximated through a

finite-differencing scheme outside of the timestepping loop.

Stability of the Unsteady Force Calculation

As discussed in Section 2.1.4, the numerical stability of unsteady filament-based methods requires the

enforcement of a timestep constraint of ∆xw /∆xc ≥ 1, where ∆xw is the distance travelled by the surface

elements each timestep, and ∆xc is the chord length of the trailing edge surface elements. Enforcement of

this timestep size constraint becomes problematic for surfaces with changing velocities along the span,

such as rotor blades. Since its second-order distributed doublet sheets can be imagined as a system

of infinitely small vortex rings, the timestepping portion of the DDE method inherently satisfies this

constraint of ∆xw /∆xc ≈ 1. In contrast, using a conventional vortex-lattice method would require an

infinite number of elements, leading to both ∆xw and ∆xc being infinitesimally small, and therefore

satisfying ∆xw /∆xc ≈ 1. As the apparent mass term represented by Eq. 3.10 is approximated after

the timestepping loop, this approximation can be made with a timestep size different than the one used

within the timestepping loop to ensure that the constraint ∆xw /∆xc ≥ 1 is maintained. This guarantees

the stability of the apparent mass approximation, independent of the velocity or distance travelled by a

surface element inside of a timestep.



3.5     Summary

The DDE method uses second-order distributed doublet sheets to model aerodynamic systems, eliminat-

ing the singularities and instabilities which are inherent in vortex filament-based potential flow methods.

Aerodynamic surfaces are modelled using planar triangular DDEs placed along the camber line. The

wake is represented as a continuous sheet of DDEs, and is grown and relaxed with the timestepping

process. Freestream and induced forces are calculated by integrating the Kutta-Joukowski theorem

across the elements, and non-circulatory forces are approximated based on the time rate of change of

the circulation. Due to the distributed nature of the DDEs and the method used for approximating

non-circulatory forces, the DDE method does not have constraints on its stability, as opposed to UVLM

or DVE-based methods.

Validation

The DDE method was evaluated through a series of four validation studies, with the methodology and

results of these studies presented within this chapter. The first study predicted the span efficiency factors

of several planar elliptical wings and quantified the impact of the discretization scheme on the results.

The second study modelled a flat plate passing through a sharp-edged gust as a means of validating the

unsteady force calculations by comparing the lift response to the analytical solution, which is given by the

Küssner function. Following this, the time-averaged thrust coefficients produced by a rotor under various

operating conditions were predicted using the DDE method and compared to experimentally-obtained

data from Ryerson University’s large subsonic wind-tunnel and rotor-test stand. Lastly, predictions of

a rotor’s transient thrust coefficient in near-edgewise flight were compared to high-fidelity CFD-based

results, constituting the validation for fully-unsteady transient analysis of rotors.



4.1     Elliptical Wing Study

In a panel density study, the predicted span efficiencies of various planar elliptical wings were compared

to classical analytical results. This study aimed to determine the effect of the discretization scheme on

the resulting force predictions. Two distinct planar elliptical wing shapes were evaluated, with both

   When using a fixed wake model, the sweep of the trailing edge impacts the shape of the wake, with a

attack of the wing (36). As a result, the elliptical wing with a straight trailing edge is expected to match

best the analytical solution of e = 1 from classical lifting line theory, which assumes a planar wake trace.

The wing with a forward-swept trailing edge, in contrast, is expected to have a span efficiency factor

greater than one due to the non-planar wake trace.

   The results of this study are presented in two sections, with the first focusing on the effect of the

and chordwise distributions on the solution. Within these panel density studies, both planar elliptical

performed at a fixed angle of attack of four degrees, resulting in a lift coefficient of CL ≈ 0.32. A fixed,

drag-free wake model was used to facilitate a direct comparison to the classical lifting line solution. The

timestep size was set equal to the root chord of the wing, and the solutions were evaluated until the

4.2     Transient Lift Response to Gusts

Apparent mass and shed circulation can contribute significantly to the resulting forces produced by an

aerodynamic surface under unsteady conditions. The DDE method uses a finite-differencing scheme

discussed in Section 3.4.3 to approximate the apparent mass term in the unsteady Kutta-Joukowski

formulation, while the shed circulation is modelled through streamwise changes in the wake doublet

strength. The purpose of this study is to validate the modelling of both of these effects, as well as to

quantify the impact of the timestep size and panel density on the resulting force predictions.

   To demonstrate the effectiveness of the DDE method for unsteady analysis, a two-dimensional flat

plate was modelled passing through a sharp-edged gust. Figure 4.8 illustrates this system, with an

infinitely-thin flat plate at zero angle of attack entering into a sharp-edged gust. The resulting transient

lift response was then compared to the analytical solution given by the Küssner function (42)(60). For

the purposes of this validation study, the gust had a magnitude equal to 5% of the freestream magnitude.




               Figure 4.8: A two-dimensional flat plate approaching a sharp-edged gust.


   Several different ∆xw /∆xc ratios were evaluated within this study. As outlined in Section 2.1.4

and illustrated in Fig. 4.8, this ratio represents the distance travelled by the flat plate relative to

the average length of a single chordwise element. These various ∆xw /∆xc ratios were obtained using

different timestep sizes and chordal element counts, with the number of chordwise elements indicated by

the parameter M .

   The results from this study are presented in Fig. 4.9, plotted alongside the analytical response given

by the Küssner function. Figure 4.9a indicates that, unlike conventional UVLM-based methods, there is

independence from the ∆xw /∆xc ratio and timestep size with the DDE method for a given number of

chordwise elements. Though the ratios for each chordwise element count in Fig. 4.9a vary by a factor of

ten, there is very little difference in the lift coefficient across the gust response as predicted by the DDE

method. For a given number of chordwise elements, the solution is largely independent of timestep size,

4.5     Summary

The DDE method was applied to four different validation studies that ultimately demonstrated its

suitability for the transient analysis of rotors operating under highly-unsteady conditions. The first

validation study evaluated planar elliptical wings, where the impacts of panel density were quantified by

observing changes in the predicted span efficiency factor. The panel density study results agreed with

the analytical solution, showing asymptotic convergence to e = 1 for an increasing number of chordwise

and spanwise elements for an elliptical wing with a straight trailing edge. A higher span efficiency factor

was predicted for an elliptical wing with a forward-swept trailing edge, as expected from the theory.

   The second validation study used the DDE method to predict the transient lift due to gusts for both

a flat plate airfoil and a finite wing, with the purpose of evaluating the apparent mass approximation and

the modelling of the shed circulation in the wake. The first analysis modelled a two-dimensional flat plate

encountering a sharp-edged gust. The results were compared with the analytical results given by the

Küssner function, and showed independence of timestep size. The error relative to the analytical solution

was inversely proportional to the number of chordwise elements used, with ten chordwise elements

resulting in a maximum percent error of 9%. This study indicates that the apparent mass approximation

can be applied to rotors in edgewise flight without numerical instabilities, and with reasonable accuracy

provided an adequate number of chordwise elements are used. These results were further supported by

the analysis of a finite rectangular wing passing through a 1-cosine gust, with the DDE-based predictions

agreeing with those found in the literature.

   The third validation study compared time-averaged rotor thrust predictions from the DDE method

to wind-tunnel experiments of a rotor operating under a range of different tip-path plane angles. Good

agreement was shown between the predicted and experimentally-obtained rotor thrust loads across most


                                                    57
cases. The maximum deviation occurred as the rotor approached a windmilling state in purely axial

flow, where the thrust produced by the rotor approaches zero.

   In the fourth study, the transient sectional thrust coefficients of a rotor in near-edgewise flight were

found using the DDE method and compared to CFD results. Both predictions agreed well in magnitude

and azimuth location, predicting the same maximum and minimum sectional thrust values and their

respective radial and azimuthal locations. The largest deviation between the methods occurred near

an azimuth location of ψ = 0, where the blade is pointed downstream. Overall, this comparison to a

high-fidelity CFD-based simulation indicates that the DDE method is well-suited for modelling rotors

operating under unsteady conditions.

Sample Applications

Two separate studies are presented in this chapter that focus on the performance of small, rigid rotors

operating under unsteady conditions. These studies offer examples of well-suited applications for the

DDE method. The first study models the impact of rolling maneuvers on the time-averaged thrust and

moments produced by a rigid rotor, as a way of quantifying the effect of rolling maneuvers on a rotor

during the flight of a quadcopter. The second study uses the DDE method to provide a detailed analysis

of the transient thrust response of two different rotors in partial and fully edgewise flow, modelling the

unsteadiness of the advancing and retreating blades and observing the trends that occur every rotation.

Both of the sample applications presented in this chapter represent analyses that are challenging or

infeasible using existing UVLM-based or DVE-based potential flow methods.



5.1     Rotor Response to Rolling

A fast, low-fidelity simulation of a rotor may simplify the flow conditions by assuming that the velocity

across the rotor disk is constant and equal to the value at the rotor hub. This assumption is reasonable

for multirotor vehicles in steady-level flight, with examples provided by Tsaltas (65) and Tsaltas and

Bramesfeld (66). However, a rotor is subjected to highly-varying kinematic velocities as the vehicle

maneuvers in-flight. These maneuvers change the magnitude and alter the distribution of the kinematic

velocity across the rotor disk, impacting the thrust and moments it produces compared to steady-level




                                                   59
flight conditions. As a result, the validity of a constant kinematic velocity assumption across the rotor

disk is questionable when analyzing a maneuvering rotor.

   This study investigates the extent to which the non-uniform kinematic velocity distribution affects the

predicted time-averaged rotor loads, as a means of testing the validity of the constant kinematic velocity

assumption. This is accomplished by analyzing a T-MOTOR 18x6.1 rotor, introduced in Section 4.3, as

it rolls at various rates and forward speeds. The time-averaged rotor loads during the roll maneuver are

compared to the steady-level flight loads, with the differences indicating the extent to which the change

in kinematic velocity across the rotor disk impacts the predicted loads.

   Analyzing the change in the thrust and moments produced by a rotor as a result of a flight maneuver

is a highly unsteady problem, and thus a well-suited application of the DDE method. Though a rotor

disk has a constant kinematic velocity distribution in steady-level flight, the rotor blades themselves are

operating under unsteady conditions due to advancing and retreating blade effects. With the addition of

a roll maneuver, this unsteadiness is compounded by changing kinematic velocities across the rotor disk.

The DDE method can quantify the extent to which the roll maneuvers impact the resulting rotor loads

due to this additional complexity. This is done by simulating the rotor in steady-level edgewise flight

until the solution converges, at which point a rolling maneuver commences at a constant roll rate. The

analysis concludes when the solution has converged again. Due to the low computational expense of the

DDE method, several operating conditions and roll rates were analyzed, and the results are compared

in this study.

   Quadrotor vehicles have two primary configurations, illustrated in Fig. 5.1. The first is known as

a square configuration, featuring two leading rotors and two trailing rotors. The second is known as

a diamond configuration, with a single leading and trailing rotor and two outboard rotors. A detailed

aerodynamic analysis of these two configurations is given by Barcelos et al. (54), which indicates highly

complex aerodynamic effects primarily due to the proximity of the rotors.

   Two variations of rolling maneuvers were analyzed for this study and discussed herein. The first

variation features the rolling motion centred about the longitudinal axis of the rotor, as encountered by

the leading and trailing rotors of the diamond configuration during a rolling maneuver. In this case,

the roll axis passes through the hubs of these leading and trailing rotors. The second variation of the

rolling maneuver is centred on an axis offset from the longitudinal axis of the rotor, as encountered by

all rotors of the square configuration and the outboard rotors on the diamond configuration, during a


                                                    60
rolling maneuver. In this case, with the roll axis offset from the rotor hub, this maneuver features both

a rotation and translation of the rotor.

the thrust and pitching moments produced by the rotor, and may have to be considered when modelling

the flight dynamics of multirotor vehicles.

5.2     Transient Unsteady Rotor Analysis

The thrust acting on a rotor in forward flight may vary with time due to advancing and retreating

blade effects acting on the individual rotor blades. While conventional helicopters compensate for these

effects with hinged blades, small multirotor vehicles typically use rigid rotors. Without compensation,

the thrust output of the rotor is cyclical, with the amplitude being linked to the forward speed and angle

of the vehicle, as well as the rotational velocity of the rotor. Transient thrust has potential implications

for both the control systems and the structural design of the rotor blades and central chassis due to

fatigue. Despite these potential implications, quantification of the time-dependent thrust on rigid rotors

at various tip-plane angles of attack is notably lacking from the literature. The purpose of this study

is to investigate and quantify the transient thrust response of small rigid rotors in forward flight under

various operating conditions through numerical predictions of unsteady blade and wake effects.

   Another important takeaway from this analysis is an apparent asymmetry to the wake structure

behind a rigid rotor in forward flight, which was noted by Misiorowski et al. (12). Figure 5.15 provides

frontal views of two T-MOTOR 18x6.1 cases, with Fig. 5.15a representing the rotor in fully-edgewise

flow at a high normalized freestream velocity, and Fig. 5.15b representing the rotor at a tip-path plane

angle of αtpp = 15◦ at a more modest normalized freestream velocity. Due to the higher blade thrust

oscillation amplitude present in the fully-edgewise case, the wake structure is highly asymmetric, with

a large increase in downwash behind the advancing blade where the individual blade thrust output is

the highest. The wake of the rotor in Fig. 5.15b, however, is more uniform, but still exhibits some

degree of asymmetry. This asymmetric velocity field downstream of the rotor disk is in conflict with

Glauert’s assumption that a rotor in high-speed forward flight has an induced downwash similar to that

of a circular wing, as discussed by Leishman (42), implying that this assumption is not necessarily valid

for rigid rotors. Knowing the wake structure behind a rigid rotor in forward flight may be of use to

multicopter designers when considering rotor-wake interactions, such as determining the optimal rotation

directions of the rotors or the best orientation of the vehicle for forward flight. Research of this nature

has been conducted by Barcelos et al. (67) and Misiorowski et al. (12).


5.2.2    Summary

The purpose of this study was to quantify the transient thrust output of rigid rotors in forward flight.

Two small rigid rotors were analyzed at various forward-flight operating conditions. Apart from the

peaks and troughs in the transient thrust coefficient due to advancing and retreating blade velocity

effects, wake effects acting on the rotor blades were also shown to affect the transient thrust coefficient

under certain operating conditions. Additionally, the analysis indicates that the wake structure behind

a rigid rotor in forward flight is asymmetric. The increased thrust output of the advancing blade leads

design plays an important role in the thrust oscillation of the rotor as the forward speed changes.

Chapter 6


Conclusions

The potential flow-based DDE method introduced in this research was designed to model complex, un-

steady systems with relaxed wakes. This was accomplished using distributed doublets with a continuous

strength distribution, which removes the potential for infinite induced velocities within the system. As

a result, the relaxed wake model of the DDE method can be coupled with a fully-unsteady analysis

framework without instabilities, whether interactions occur between the wake elements themselves or

the aerodynamic surfaces and wake. Due to the higher-order distributed nature of the elements, the

unsteady apparent mass approximation does not have stability constraints limiting the timestep size, as

is the case with conventional UVLM or DVE-based methods.

   The DDE method was evaluated using a series of four validation studies. A panel density study

using planar elliptical wings showed that comparatively few elements were needed to match the classical

lifting-line solution to an elliptical wing’s span efficiency when using cosine spanwise element spacing.

The unsteady capabilities of the DDE method were tested by modelling an airfoil subjected to a shape-

edged gust. The results confirmed that the apparent mass approximation’s stability is not constrained

by timestep size, though the accuracy is related to the number of chordwise elements used to model the

airfoil. The DDE method was then used to predict the time-averaged thrust of a small, rigid rotor at

various freestream advance ratios and tip-path plane angles, agreeing well with experimentally-obtained

data. As a final validation study, the transient sectional thrust of a rotor blade operating at a specific

angle of attack and freestream advance ratio was predicted, with the results matching those obtained

via a fully unsteady, transient CFD-based analysis.

                                                   78
   The results of two studies using the DDE-based methods are provided in Chapter 5, both focusing

on the unsteady analysis of rigid rotors. The first application used the DDE method to simulate the

thrust and moments produced by a rotor in fully edgewise flight as it was subjected to various rolling

maneuvers. When rolling about the longitudinal axis of the rotor, the maneuver affected the rolling

moment coefficient by anywhere from 2-14%, depending on the roll rate and direction. The additional

axial velocity from rolling about an axis offset from the longitudinal axis of the rotor affected the thrust

and pitching moment produced in the order of 5-10% across a range of roll rates.

   In the second study, the DDE method was used to predict the transient thrust output of two different

rotors in forward flight, operating under a range of different normalized freestream velocities and tip-

path plane angles. The rotors’ transient thrust oscillated with an amplitude of up to 30% of the mean

total thrust. The thrust output of the individual rotor blades was predicted to oscillate by up to 50% of

the mean rotor thrust under certain conditions, underscoring the need for a fully unsteady framework

when analyzing rotors in forward flight. Findings regarding transient oscillations offer additional context

for structural and control systems designers, who currently rely predominantly on time-averaged rotor

loads due to the inaccessible nature of this type of aerodynamic analysis.

   Applying the DDE method to these unsteady systems opens up new possibilities in design analysis,

exploration, and optimization. This was made possible through leveraging the advantages of distributed

doublet sheets within a novel full-unsteady lifting surface framework. The DDE method offers clear

advantages in labour and computational cost compared to CFD-based analysis, filling the capability gap

in computational methods for today’s complex and unconventional aircraft configurations.



6.1     Future Work

There are several extensions to the DDE method which will become the focus of future work. To further

reduce the computational expense of the method, an adaptive panelling method could be implemented

within the wake relaxation procedure, regionally varying the panel density based on the wake shape.

This procedure could help maintain the geometric fidelity of the wake, particularly around the shed tip

vortex, while mitigating the computational expense of unnecessary elements in the more planar regions

of the wake.

   The distributed doublet induction model outlined by Johnson (23) can be extended to include a


                                                    79
curved panel approximation, which allows the doublet sheets to be modelled as non-planar surfaces. The

DDE method could be modified to incorporate this approximation, modelling the aerodynamic surfaces

using non-planar quadrilateral elements. This capability would reduce the necessary number of surface

elements while increasing the fidelity of the camber line modelling. The extent to which the inclusion of

curved panels would benefit the DDE method is currently unknown and warrants investigation.

   Further verification of the unsteady DDE force predictions will be conducted, particularly in compari-

son with classical theoretical unsteady aerodynamic solutions that incorporate the pitching and plunging

of lifting surfaces. The suitability of the DDE method for aeroelastic and aeroacoustic predictions will be

evaluated through verification studies, likely through the analysis of helicopter rotors where experimental

data is readily available.

   In terms of applications, the DDE method will be coupled with an optimization process to design

rigid rotors for forward flight, leveraging the transient predictive capabilities demonstrated in Sections

4.4 and 5.2. An exploratory study into rigid rotor blade design could help designers understand the

influence of the design parameters on the resulting thrust output under oscillating advancing-retreating

conditions. In addition to rotor optimization, the DDE method will be applied to the analysis and design

of integrated aircraft systems such as multirotor drones and urban air mobility vehicles, providing a low-

cost alternative to high-fidelity CFD-based methods.
